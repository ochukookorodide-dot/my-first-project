(0:22) *Title card: Neanderthals weren't wiped out. They were outnumbered.*
Today I'll show you what happened when Neanderthals met us, why a small group can disappear without being wiped out, and what they left behind inside you.

(0:33) *Simple map of Europe and western Asia, a brown blob labelled Neanderthals.*
Let's start with the story most of us were told. Neanderthals lived across Europe and western Asia for hundreds of thousands of years.

(0:43) *Arrow from Africa onto the map, stick humans walking in a line.*
Then modern humans spread out of Africa. And roughly forty thousand years ago, the last Neanderthals were gone.

(0:51) *A tiny cinema screen: stickmen with spears, one big winner, one loser.*
The usual explanation sounds like a movie. We were smarter, we were stronger, we won. Maybe we even fought them.

(0:59) *A stickman Neanderthal making a stone tool, another burying someone with flowers, a small fire between them.*
And that movie often casts Neanderthals as dim brutes. But the evidence says otherwise. They made sophisticated tools, they used fire, and they cared for their injured. These were people, not extras in our story.

(1:14) *Timeline bar, 41 and 39 marked in thousands of years. A shaded overlap zone appears.*
But when researchers dated sites across Europe, the picture looked less like a sudden defeat, and more like a slow fade. A 2014 study of forty sites found the Neanderthal tool culture ended between about forty-one and thirty-nine thousand years ago.

(1:30) *The overlap zone grows. Label: 2,600 to 5,400 years together.*
And it found that Neanderthals and modern humans overlapped for somewhere between twenty-six hundred and fifty-four hundred years.

(1:38) *A Roman helmet next to a calendar. Label: Rome began about 2,000 years ago.*
Even the shortest of those estimates is longer than the time since the Roman Empire began.

(1:45) *Two stickmen houses side by side, a fence between them, someone waving.*
Thousands of years of living as neighbours. And neighbours do more than fight.

(1:51) *A lab drawing: a stickman scientist with a bandana and a test tube.*
In 2010, a team led by Svante Pääbo published a draft of the Neanderthal genome, and compared it with the DNA of living people.

(2:01) *A world map. Africa is plain. Everywhere else has a small brown dot. Label: about 2 percent.*
They found Neanderthal DNA in people outside Africa. Roughly two percent in most estimates, though early studies gave a range from one to four.

(2:11) *A very long strip of tiny DNA letters with a brown chunk highlighted. Text: tens of millions of letters.*
Two percent sounds tiny. But the human genome has about three billion letters, so two percent is tens of millions of them. Not a trace. A real inheritance.

(2:23) *Two columns of DNA letters, one African, one non-African, with a brown block highlighted in the second.*
How do they know? They compare living people from different parts of the world with the Neanderthal genome. Stretches of DNA that match Neanderthals, but are largely missing in people with recent African ancestry, are the fingerprints of mixing.

(2:39) *A stick couple, one with a heavy brow, holding a baby.*
That can only happen one way. Neanderthals and our ancestors met, and they had children.

(2:45) *Timeline: 65 to 45, in thousands of years ago, a heart icon in the middle.*
Studies place this mixing somewhere around forty-five to sixty-five thousand years ago, depending on the method, as modern humans spread out of Africa.

(2:55) *A cave drawing with a skeleton inside. Label: Romania.*
One skeleton shows just how real it was. A man from a cave in Romania, who lived around forty thousand years ago.

(3:04) *Cave with bones inside. Text: Pestera cu Oase, the cave with bones.*
The cave is called Peștera cu Oase, Romanian for the cave with bones. A jaw found there belonged to an early modern human, and its DNA was about to surprise everyone.

(3:17) *Genome bar: 6 to 9 percent coloured brown.*
In 2015, scientists found that six to nine percent of his genome was Neanderthal.

(3:23) *Family tree going up four to six boxes, with a skull at the top.*
Some stretches of his DNA were so long and unbroken that a Neanderthal ancestor must have been only four to six generations back. A great-great-grandparent, or a few generations further.

(3:36) *The family tree fades out and a question mark appears at the bottom.*
Here's the twist. His line doesn't seem to have survived. He shows no close link to later Europeans. So mixed families existed, and some of them still faded away.

(3:48) *A single branch of a tree with a brown leaf, ending in a stub.*
The researchers think he may have belonged to an early group of modern humans that mixed with Neanderthals, but contributed little to the Europeans who came later. A branch that mixed, and then ended.

(4:02) *Timeline stretching back to 200,000 years, several small hearts along it.*
And it wasn't a single moment. A 2024 study found repeated gene flow between Neanderthals and modern humans over around two hundred thousand years.

(4:12) *Music drops out. Text: So they met.*
So they met. They had children. That changes the question.

(4:17) *Two boxes. Box one: wiped out, with an empty gene bar. Box two: absorbed, with a brown stripe in the gene bar.*
Here's a simple test. If Neanderthals had been wiped out without ever having children with us, we'd expect no Neanderthal DNA in anyone alive today. If they mixed with us, we'd expect exactly what we see. Their DNA, in small amounts, scattered through billions of people.

(4:35) *Text: How did we beat them? crossed out. Text: What happens when a small group keeps mixing with a big one?*
Not, how did we beat them? But, what happens when a small group keeps mixing with a much bigger one?

(4:44) *Two groups of stickmen: five on the left labelled small, twenty on the right labelled big. Arrows between them.*
Imagine two groups. One is small and scattered. The other is bigger, and growing. Now imagine they keep meeting, generation after generation.

(4:53) *A few mixed stickmen, half brown, half white, appear between them.*
Every child of a mixed couple carries pieces of both. But the bigger group keeps having more children, and those children keep mixing with the bigger group.

(5:05) *Text: Toy example, made-up numbers. 100 small dots on the left, 1000 big dots on the right. A few dots drift across.*
Here's a toy example, with made-up numbers. Say the small group has a hundred people and the big group has a thousand. Each generation, a few young people from the small group join the big one and have families there. The big group barely notices. The small group loses people it can't replace, until there aren't enough left to count as a group.

(5:30) *The brown colour in the small group gets paler across four panels.*
Over time, the smaller group's lineage gets watered down. Its distinct traits fade. Not because anyone was killed, but because with every generation, more of the people alive come from the bigger side.

(5:44) *A glass of dark brown juice, with water pouring in until it is pale.*
Scientists call this genetic dilution. And a 2025 modelling study found that repeated small waves of modern humans arriving could replace almost all Neanderthal ancestry within roughly ten to thirty thousand years.

(5:57) *Label on screen: A model, not proof.*
Remember, that's a model. It shows what's possible. It doesn't prove what happened.

(6:03) *Text: A hint from the genes.*
But the genetics gives us a few reasons to take the idea seriously, so let's look at two.

(6:11) *A very small stick camp, three tents, lots of empty space around it.*
Why would Neanderthals be the smaller side? Studies of their DNA point to small, isolated populations.

(6:18) *A tiny stick camp, a storm cloud over it, one stickman looking at an empty cave.*
Think about what that means for a group. Fewer people means fewer mates, less genetic variety, and a bad winter or a run of bad luck hits much harder.

(6:30) *Little red cracks appear in a drawing of a stick figure's DNA.*
And in small populations, mildly harmful mutations pile up more easily, because natural selection is weaker. One simulation study estimated that the average Neanderthal may have been somewhat less fit than the average modern human.

(6:44) *Slow zoom on a stick figure looking at a mirror. The mirror shows a Neanderthal.*
So what did Neanderthals leave behind in us? More than you might expect.

(6:50) *A chromosome drawn like a fat X with a number 3 and a tiny skull.*
The best-known example is a stretch of DNA on chromosome three. In 2020, Pääbo's team showed that the version of it that raises the risk of severe COVID-19 came from Neanderthals.

(7:03) *Map: Europe with one in six stickmen coloured brown. South Asia with half coloured brown.*
About one in six Europeans carries it. In South Asia, around half of people do.

(7:09) *A second chromosome with a green tick.*
In 2021, the same group found a different Neanderthal stretch linked to protection against severe disease.

(7:16) *A stickman shrugging, a tiny question mark beside a virus drawing.*
To be clear, that doesn't mean Neanderthals made you sick. It means pieces of an ancient immune system are still at work inside us, and scientists are still figuring out exactly how.

(7:29) *A gold medal drawn in yellow with a stickman in a bandana holding it.*
In 2022, Svante Pääbo won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his work on the genomes of extinct humans, including showing that interbreeding really happened.

(7:41) *A second skull drawn next to the Neanderthal one, labelled Denisovan, with a question mark.*
His team also identified a previously unknown group of ancient humans, the Denisovans. So Neanderthals weren't a one-off. Our ancestors mixed with more than one ancient group.

(7:52) *A stickman with a bandana peers through a magnifying glass at a tiny skull.*
And remember, all of this comes from DNA that survived tens of thousands of years, broken into tiny pieces and mixed with contamination. Reading it at all is one of the great technical achievements of modern science.

(8:07) *Three icons in a row with checkmarks: shared land, shared children, one group fading.*
Let's pause and recap. Neanderthals and modern humans shared the same lands for thousands of years. They had children together, more than once. And when a smaller population keeps meeting a larger one, it can fade away without a single battle. That's the case for outnumbered.

(8:26) *Text: Now, to be fair.*
Now, I want to be fair. Outnumbered isn't the whole story, and nobody knows the exact mix of causes.

(8:34) *A thermometer and an ice block drawn beside a tiny camp.*
Researchers have also pointed to climate swings, which could hit small, scattered groups hard.

(8:40) *Stickmen arguing over a rabbit. A sick stickman lying down.*
Competition for food and land may have mattered. Disease may have mattered. And small groups are fragile. A few bad years, and a whole population can collapse.

(8:52) *A pie chart with three unlabelled slices and a big question mark.*
So it's probably not either-or. A shrinking population mixing with a growing one fits the evidence well. But the exact balance is still debated.

(9:02) *The Romanian skeleton again, with a faded family tree behind it.*
And the Romanian skeleton is a reminder that even mixed lineages could vanish.

(9:08) *Two panels: left, a stickman with a trophy standing over another; right, a blended group. A cross over the left panel.*
What the genetics does show is that the old picture, a clean victory where one species replaced another, is too simple.

(9:16) *A stickman scratching his head next to a list: Exact numbers? Exact dates? Exact causes?*
There is a lot we still don't know. The exact size of Neanderthal populations, the exact dates of every meeting, and the exact mix of causes are all still being worked out, and new fossils and new DNA keep changing the details.

(9:34) *Three simple icons in a row: handshake, mix, fade. With arrows between them.*
But the outline is getting clearer. Neanderthals and modern humans met. They mixed, repeatedly. And a smaller population faded into a larger one, leaving its genes behind.

(9:45) *Dark screen. Text: Did Neanderthals go extinct?*
So, did Neanderthals go extinct?

(9:48) *A Neanderthal stickman waves goodbye, fading.*
As a distinct people, with their own bodies, their own tools and their own communities, yes. They're gone.

(9:55) *A dictionary page drawn with a wobbly line. Word: extinct. A small footnote star.*
In biology, extinct usually means a lineage ends with no descendants. Neanderthals are an odd case. The Neanderthal people ended, but through mixing, pieces of them carry on. So extinct is true, and also incomplete.

(10:10) *A world map filled with tiny brown dots, the same as before.*
But as a set of genes, they were never fully lost. They are inside the people who walked out of Africa and spread across the world.

(10:21) *A stick family tree reaching back, with a brown stickman on one branch.*
Almost everyone with ancestry from outside Africa has a family tree that runs through a Neanderthal somewhere. Your story and theirs were never really separate.

(10:31) *Back to the opening stickman with the DNA squiggle. A Neanderthal skull appears next to him, smiling.*
So next time someone says we wiped out the Neanderthals, you can say, it's more complicated. We outnumbered them, and somewhere along the way, we became partly them.

If you want to see how you'd cope in their world, the next video asks, could you survive a week among Neanderthals? I'll see you there.
